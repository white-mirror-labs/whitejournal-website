How to Start Journaling, and Keep Going

Most people do not stop journaling because they run out of things to say. They stop because the first version of the habit asked too much of them.

Start smaller than you think

The most common mistake is beginning with a promise: a page a day, every day, forever. A habit built on a promise breaks the first time life gets busy, and the broken promise becomes the reason to stop.

Begin with three lines instead. One about how you feel. One about what happened. One about what you noticed. Three lines take two minutes, and two minutes survive almost any day.

Pick a moment, not a time

"Every evening at nine" competes with everything else that happens at nine. Attach writing to something you already do: the first coffee, the train home, the last few minutes before you sleep. The existing habit carries the new one.

Write by hand when you can

Writing on paper is slower than typing, and that is useful. Slowness gives a thought time to finish. A paper journal also has no notifications, so the two minutes stay yours. Many people find it easier to be honest on a page nobody else can open.

Use a question when the page feels empty

A blank page is intimidating. A question is not. Try one of these:

  • What am I feeling right now, in one word?
  • What took most of my energy today?
  • What did I avoid, and why?
  • What would I like to do differently tomorrow?

For more, see our journaling prompts for self-reflection.

Missing a day is part of the habit

You will skip days. Everyone does. The rule that keeps a journal alive is simple: never skip twice. When you come back, do not write about why you were away. Just write today's three lines.

Read back once a week

The value of a journal grows when you read it. Once a week, look over the last seven entries. You will start to see what keeps returning: the same worry, the same kind of good day, the same trigger. That is where self-awareness begins, not on the day you write, but on the day you notice the pattern.

A simple structure that grows with you

When three lines feel easy, add structure. Our guide to reflective journaling walks through the M.I.R.R.O.R. method: notice the mood, name it, reflect, reframe, observe the pattern, and choose one next step.

Common questions

How long should I journal each day?

Two to ten minutes is enough. Consistency matters far more than length.

Is it better to journal in the morning or at night?

Whichever you can repeat. Mornings suit planning and intention; evenings suit reflection on the day.

Should I use a notebook or an app?

Both work. Paper helps you slow down and stay private; an app helps you see patterns over time. Many people use both.
